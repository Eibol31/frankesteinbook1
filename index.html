Chapter 02 · Literary Inspiration

The Author: Mary Shelley

The mind behind the modern Prometheus

Mary Shelley was a British novelist, essayist and short-story writer. Born in 1797 to two radical thinkers (William Godwin and Mary Wollstonecraft), she grew up surrounded by books and political debate.

In 1816, during a stay near Lake Geneva with Lord Byron and Percy Bysshe Shelley, she conceived the idea for Frankenstein during a ghost-story writing challenge. What started as a terrifying tale became a profound reflection on life, death, and creation.

Chapter 03 · Literary Inspiration

The Work: Frankenstein

A novel that defies the boundaries of life

Published anonymously in 1818, Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus is a novel that blends Gothic horror with philosophical inquiry, exploring the boundaries of life and death.

  • TitleFrankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus
  • First Publication1818 (London)
  • Literary FormNovel
  • Narrative StructureFrame narrative with multiple first-person narrators
Why it matters: Frankenstein is widely considered one of the earliest examples of science fiction, asking timeless questions about the dangers of unchecked scientific ambition.
Chapter 04 · Literary Inspiration

The Literary Movement

Romanticism and Gothic Fiction

Frankenstein belongs to two overlapping literary currents that shaped the 19th century:

Romanticism

A movement emphasising emotion, imagination, individual experience, and the sublime power of nature. Mary Shelley was deeply connected to the Romantic poets of her time.

Gothic Fiction

A tradition exploring fear, mystery, isolation, psychological tension, and transgression of natural or moral limits. The novel's dark laboratories and stormy landscapes are classic Gothic elements.

Chapter 07 · Key Elements

Setting & Conflict

The world and the struggle

The environment and the central struggle shape the gothic atmosphere of the novel and drive its philosophical inquiry.

Setting

The novel spans Geneva, the Swiss Alps, Germany, England, Scotland, and the Arctic. The sublime landscapes (mountains, glaciers, ice) reflect the Romantic concept of nature as beautiful, powerful, and overwhelming.

Central Conflict

The novel is driven by two primary conflicts:

  • External Conflict: The struggle between Victor Frankenstein and his creation. The Creature demands recognition and companionship, while Victor seeks to escape or destroy what he has made.
  • Internal Conflict: Victor's internal struggle between his scientific ambition and his moral responsibility.
Analysis: The conflict is not a simple battle between good and evil. It is a conflict between creation and abandonment, knowledge and ethics, and ultimately, between what society sees and what it refuses to see.
Chapter 08 · Full Text

Read Book

Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus

About the book. The novel is built as a story within a story: Walton's letters frame Victor Frankenstein's narrative, and inside Victor's narrative is the Creature's own account. This reading guide follows the structure of the edition provided (4 letters and 24 chapters), grouped into the book's three volumes, with a short overview of each part and a summary of each letter or chapter.

Prologue: Walton's Letters

In brief: An explorer writing to his sister meets a dying stranger on the ice and persuades him to tell his story.

Letter 1: Captain Robert Walton writes to his sister, Margaret Saville, in England to say he has arrived safely in Russia and his plan to sail toward the North Pole is under way. He hopes to discover the secret of the compass needle and open a new sea route, but above all he wants to see a land no one has reached.

Letter 2: Walton reports he has hired a vessel and is gathering sailors. He confesses he has one unsatisfied need: a friend. He longs for a companion of cultivated mind to approve or correct his plans.

Letter 3: A short, hurried letter. Walton reports he is safe, well advanced on his voyage, and in good spirits. He declares that nothing can stop the determined heart and resolved will of man.

Letter 4 (The stranger on the ice): On July 31st, the ship was almost surrounded by ice. The crew found a sledge with a single man, nearly dead from exposure. Before coming aboard, the stranger asked where the ship was bound. The crew revived him, and the stranger became the friend Walton wished for. The stranger warns him about the "intoxicating draught" of ambition and agrees to tell Walton his life, which Walton resolves to write down.

Part I: Victor's Story — Creation (Chapters 1-7)

In brief: Victor's happy childhood in Geneva gives way to obsessive study at Ingolstadt, where he brings a creature to life and is immediately horrified by it.

Chapter 4 - The secret of life: Victor throws himself into chemistry. After two years he decides to find the cause of life itself. He studies anatomy and decay, spending days in vaults until he discovers how to bestow animation on lifeless matter. He decides to make his being gigantic, about eight feet tall.

Chapter 5 - The creation: On a dreary night in November, Victor infuses the spark of life into the body. At once the beauty he had planned dissolves. Breathless with horror, he rushes from the room. He wakes to find the creature standing by his bed, its hand stretched out. Victor flees into the streets.

Chapter 6 - Recovery: As Victor recovers from a nervous fever, Clerval hands him a letter from Elizabeth. Victor avoids all sight of chemical instruments and gradually regains his spirits, helped by the arrival of spring.

Chapter 7 - William's death: A letter from Alphonse reaches Victor with terrible news: William, his youngest brother, has been found strangled. In a violent thunderstorm, a flash of lightning reveals the creature near the spot, and Victor knows it is the murderer. He decides not to tell, certain no one would believe him.

Part II: The Creature's Story — Rejection (Chapters 8-16)

In brief: An innocent woman is executed, and in the mountains the Creature confronts his maker with his own account of what loneliness and rejection have made of him.

Chapter 8 - Justine's trial: Justine is condemned for William's murder based on circumstantial evidence. She confesses falsely because her confessor threatened her. Justine is executed the next morning.

Chapter 9 - Despair: Victor sinks into a dark guilt. To escape his thoughts, he sets off alone on a journey into the mountains of the Chamonix valley. The sublime scenery briefly restores some peace.

Chapter 10 - The meeting on the glacier: Crossing the sea of ice, Victor sees the creature. The creature answers calmly and eloquently: he was benevolent and good, misery made him a fiend. He asks Victor to listen and follows him to a hut on the ice.

Chapter 11 - Awakening: The creature describes the confusion of his first moments. He left Victor's apartment, wandered through the forest, and suffered from cold and hunger. When he entered a village, the inhabitants fled in terror or attacked him.

Chapter 12-14 - Learning from the cottagers: The creature observes the cottage's inhabitants: De Lacey, Felix, and Agatha. He secretly gathers wood for them and learns their language. He learns about their history and the arrival of Safie.

Chapter 15 - Books, the journal, and the first rejection: The creature finds books including Paradise Lost and Victor's journal. He resolves to try to win the cottagers' love, but Felix beats him and drives him away.

Chapter 16 - Rejection and revenge: Despair and fury made him want to destroy everything. He burned the cottage. Near Geneva, he strangled William and put a portrait in Justine's dress to cast the blame on her. He ends by asking Victor to make him a female as hideous as himself.

Part III: Vengeance and Ruin (Chapters 17-24)

In brief: Victor's broken promise costs him everyone he loves, and the pursuit that follows carries both creator and creation to the ends of the earth.

Chapter 17 - The bargain: The creature promises that if Victor makes a female, the two will leave Europe. Fearing for his family, Victor agrees.

Chapter 18-19 - The journey to England and Scotland: Victor travels to England with Clerval to learn from scientists. He leaves Clerval and sails to the Orkneys to begin his work in a squalid hut.

Chapter 20 - The destroyed creation: Victor considers that the female might be more malignant than the male. He tears the unfinished body to pieces. The creature swears revenge: "I shall be with you on your wedding-night."

Chapter 21 - Accused of murder: Victor is brought before a magistrate. He is taken to see the body and recognizes his closest friend, Henry Clerval. Victor falls into a delirium and fever that lasts two months.

Chapter 22-23 - The wedding night: Victor decides to marry Elizabeth at once and confront his enemy alone. After the ceremony, they sail across Lake Geneva. Victor hears a piercing scream and finds Elizabeth lifeless. At the open window he sees the creature. Victor's father, shocked by Elizabeth's death, dies within days.

Chapter 24 - The pursuit and the end: Victor leaves Geneva to hunt the creature. He pursues him toward the Arctic until Walton's ship rescues him. Victor dies, telling Walton to seek happiness in tranquility. The creature enters the cabin, grieves over the body, and tells Walton of his own anguish, saying his crimes were the product of loneliness. He leaps from the window onto an ice raft and vanishes into the darkness.

Main Themes

  • Responsibility of the creator: Victor's real crime is not the act of creation but abandoning what he made.
  • Ambition and the limits of knowledge: Walton and Victor both pursue glory at the cost of those around them.
  • Isolation and the need for companionship: The Creature is made monstrous by rejection.
  • Nature versus science: Mountains, storms, and lakes soothe and warn, while unrestrained experiment brings ruin.
  • Appearance and judgment: The Creature's kindness is ignored because of how he looks.
  • Revenge and its cost: The pursuit consumes both Victor and the Creature.

  • Henry ClervalVictor's loyal childhood friend, a humanist and lover of poetry and languages.

  • William and ErnestVictor's younger brothers; William is the creature's first victim.

Victor Frankenstein

The Creator · Protagonist

Description

A young man from a distinguished Genevan family. He is intelligent, passionate, and curious, with an intense thirst for knowledge that becomes obsession. As a child he is deeply loved and sheltered; as a student he isolates himself and ruins his health to pursue his goal. He is capable of deep affection but also of cowardice and self-absorption.

Role in the story

He discovers how to give life to inanimate matter and creates the Creature, but flees in horror at its appearance and abandons it. Out of fear that no one will believe him, he stays silent while Justine is condemned for William's murder. He later promises to make a companion for the Creature, destroys the work, and so triggers the Creature's revenge on those he loves.

Fate

Having lost his brother, friend, bride, and father, he spends his final days chasing the Creature to the Arctic and dies aboard Walton's ship, still uncertain whether he did right.

The Creature

The Creation · Antagonist

Description

A being about eight feet tall, assembled by Victor, with yellow skin that barely covers the muscles beneath, watery eyes, and black lips. Despite his terrifying look he is intelligent, sensitive, and eloquent. He teaches himself language and morality by secretly watching the De Lacey family and by reading Paradise Lost, Plutarch's Lives, and The Sorrows of Werter.

Role in the story

Rejected by everyone he meets, including his creator, he turns from a gentle, benevolent being into a vengeful one. He kills William, frames Justine, demands a female companion from Victor, and, when Victor destroys her, kills Clerval and Elizabeth. He represents the consequences of abandonment and the injustice of judging by appearance.

Fate

After Victor's death he grieves over the body, takes responsibility for his crimes, and vows to burn himself on a funeral pyre in the far north.

Robert Walton

The Frame Narrator

Description

An English sea captain in his twenties, self-educated, ambitious, and passionately determined to reach the North Pole and make a great discovery. He writes to his sister, Margaret Saville, and admits he is lonely and wishes for a friend who shares his tastes.

Role in the story

He rescues Victor, becomes his friend, and records his story. His own ambition mirrors Victor's, and Victor's warning gives him a chance to avoid the same mistake. He is also the one who witnesses the Creature's final confession.

Fate

When his crew demands it, he agrees to turn the ship south if it is freed from the ice, and he survives to write the end of the story.

Elizabeth Lavenza

The Emotional Centre

Description

A fair-haired, gentle, and calm girl, the orphaned daughter of a Milanese nobleman and a German mother, taken in by the Frankenstein at Lake Como. She is loving, compassionate, and loyal, and she often acts as a moral counterweight to Victor.

Role in the story

She grows up as Victor's closest companion and eventually his fiancée. She defends Justine at the trial and writes letters that keep Victor informed about home. Her marriage to Victor is delayed by his task for the Creature.

Fate

She is strangled by the Creature on her wedding night, fulfilling his threat.

Henry Clerval

The Best Friend

Description

A cheerful, sociable, imaginative young man, the son of a Geneva merchant. He loves poetry, romances of chivalry, and languages, and he studies Oriental languages at Ingolstadt. He represents the humane, balanced figure Victor fails to be.

Role in the story

He nurses Victor through his long fever after the creation and accompanies him to England and Scotland. His company comforts Victor, and his death in Ireland is one of the Creature's most painful acts of revenge.

Fate

He is strangled by the Creature, and Victor is briefly accused of the murder.

Alphonse Frankenstein

The Father

Description

A respected Geneva official and a devoted, patient, and loving father. He is practical and rational and prefers measured responses to extreme ones.

Role in the story

He supports Victor through his grief, arranges for Clerval to join him in England, and urges the marriage to Elizabeth. He is also the one who comes to Ireland to free Victor.

Fate

He dies of grief a few days after Elizabeth's murder.

Caroline Beaufort

The Mother

Description

A kind and compassionate woman, daughter of a merchant ruined by poverty, who supported her father until Alphonse found her. She is generous and gentle and adopts Elizabeth.

Role in the story

Her death from scarlet fever, caught from Elizabeth, is Victor's first great loss. In his dream after the creation, Elizabeth turns into her corpse.

Fate

She dies before Victor leaves for Ingolstadt, asking that he and Elizabeth marry.

Justine Moritz

The Tragic Servant

Description

A girl taken into the Frankenstein household after a childhood of mistreatment by her mother. She is devout, honest, grateful, and loved like a sister by Elizabeth.

Role in the story

Falsely accused of William's murder when the miniature portrait is found in her pocket, she is condemned. Pressured by her confessor, she makes a false confession. Victor, who knows she is innocent, does not speak up.

Fate

She is executed, and her death deepens Victor's remorse.

William Frankenstein

The First Victim

Description

A lively, affectionate, and beautiful child, loved by the whole family. He wears a miniature portrait of his mother around his neck.

Role in the story

He is the Creature's first murder victim. His death starts the chain of events that leads to Justine's execution and Victor's guilt.

Fate

He is strangled near Geneva.

Ernest Frankenstein

The Younger Brother

Description

A quieter brother who is mentioned mainly in letters and conversations at home. He wishes to follow a profession and is a comfort to his father.

Role in the story

He is a minor figure, but he remains one of the few members of the family still alive at the end of Victor's story.

Fate

He survives the novel's events as far as the narrative tells.

Professor Waldman

The Mentor · Ingolstadt

Description

A kind, gracious, middle-aged man with a gentle voice who speaks about science with respect and enthusiasm.

Role in the story

His lecture on the achievements of modern chemists inspires Victor to pursue the science that leads to the creation.

Fate

He appears only in Victor's student years.

Professor Krempe

The Critic · Ingolstadt

Description

A short, coarse-voiced man with an unpleasant appearance, rigid and scornful.

Role in the story

He dismisses Victor's reading of Agrippa, Paracelsus, and Albertus Magnus as wasted time, which at first discourages Victor from the sciences.

Fate

He later praises Victor's progress.

The De Lacey Family

The Cottagers

Description

A once-prosperous French family in exile: a blind and gentle father, a brave and devoted son (Felix), and a sweet, kind daughter (Agatha). They live in poverty and love each other deeply.

Role in the story

The Creature secretly observes them and learns from their example. De Lacey treats him kindly, but Felix, seeing him, beats him and drives him away, which pushes the Creature toward revenge.

Fate

They leave the cottage after the attack, and the Creature burns it.

Safie

The Eastern Visitor

Description

A young woman from the East, daughter of a Turkish merchant and a Christian Arab mother. She is warm, independent, and brave, and she flees her father to join Felix.

Role in the story

Her arrival and French lessons give the Creature the chance to learn language, and her story shows loyalty and love.

Fate

She remains with the De Laceys until they leave.

Mr. Kirwin

The Magistrate

Description

An old, benevolent man, calm and fair.

Role in the story

He examines Victor after Clerval's murder, shows him kindness during his illness, and sends for his father.

Fate

He appears only in Ireland.
